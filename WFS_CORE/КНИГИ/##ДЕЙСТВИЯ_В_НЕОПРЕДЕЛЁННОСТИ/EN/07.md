**Chapter 2. The Arsenal That Does Not Fire**

**2.1. The Shelf with Tools**

In a leader's office there is a shelf holding books with formulas: game theory, Bayesian networks, the Monte Carlo method, multi-criteria analysis, the Wald, Savage, and Hurwicz criteria, the concepts of VUCA, Cynefin, and OODA. The shelf impresses. It creates the impression that here are collected tools that only need to be taken and applied, since everything is thought through and calculated. Yet at the moment when a decision must be made immediately, the library of methods remains unused. Methods are not applied automatically; formulas are not transferred into reality without the subject's effort. The tools are present, and the decision is not.

The reason is not that the methods are bad. The reason is that they were created for a different subject and for a different state.

**2.2. Catalog of Methods: What Science Offers**

Modern decision theory has accumulated dozens of methods. All of them attempt either to reduce uncertainty or to adapt to it. Systematization shows that behind the variety of names stand several groups, and each has a weakness that becomes obvious in the light of #WFS.

**Deterministic methods.** These include classical optimization, linear programming, and the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). They assume that the future is known or can be precisely calculated. The weakness of these methods in the real world manifests as the "hedgehog error": a strategy that works only under one outcome leads to catastrophic losses when the forecast deviates. Deterministic methods ignore uncertainty rather than work with it. In terms of #BI, they are applicable only in closed systems with complete data; in open systems — life, the market, society — they produce false confidence.

**Probabilistic methods.** Monte Carlo, Bayesian networks, expected utility theory, and decision trees require the subject to assign probabilities to possible outcomes. Here a fundamental contradiction arises: where do probabilities come from if the situation is a moment of uncertainty? Methods created to work with uncertainty require knowledge of probabilities that do not exist in uncertainty. Under conditions of "unknown unknowns" they do not work; they only create an illusion of control. #BI assessment: applicable when probabilities are known, that is, in the case of risk, not uncertainty; under conditions of radical uncertainty, useless.

**Behavioral and heuristic methods.** Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, heuristics, Bezos's "two doors," and free rolling take into account that the real person is not rational. The weakness of these methods is that they are fast but unreliable: they give a satisfactory decision, not a true one. They work with distortions but do not eliminate their source. #BI assessment: useful as a compromise, but not as a decision, since they do not provide a binary criterion.

**Methods for working with deep uncertainty (DMDU).** Refusal of a single forecast, construction of multiple scenarios, robust decisions, and adaptive policies represent a step forward compared to probabilistic methods. The weakness of these methods is that they work with the external environment but not with the internal state of the decision-maker, and they do not eliminate the archetypes that distort perception even with good scenarios. #BI assessment: a step forward, but still external with respect to the subject.

**Integrated approaches.** ML + operations research, Real Options Theory, and adaptive decision support systems require data and computational resources. They do not work under conditions where there are no data, and they do not take into account the operator's state. #BI assessment: powerful tools, but for a world where the subject is already honest, whole, and coherent.

**Selection criteria under complete uncertainty.** The Wald (maximin), Savage (minimax regret), Hurwicz (compromise), and Laplace (equiprobability) criteria formalize subjective preferences but do not provide truth. They answer the question "how to choose?" but not the question "what is?" #BI assessment: useful as auxiliary tools, but not as a replacement for #BI.

**2.3. Four Levels of Uncertainty**

The table shows where exactly the boundary of the arsenal's applicability passes.

| Level | Name | Characteristic | Methods |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deterministic | The future is known | Classical optimization, AHP |
| 2 | Risk | Probabilities are known | Monte Carlo, Bayesian networks |
| 3 | Uncertainty | Probabilities are unknown | Heuristics, scenarios |
| 4 | Ignorance | It is unknown even what exactly is unknown | #WFS (work with the internal state) |

Modern methods work at the first three levels. At the fourth they are powerless, because there are no data to rely on. And this level is the most important: the price of error at it is highest, and it is precisely at it that the president, general, leader, doctor, judge, and rescuer act, who cannot wait for complete data and must decide now, with what they have.

**2.4. Three Root Defects of the Arsenal**

**The first defect** consists in working with probabilities, not with truth. All methods — from Wald to Bayes — operate with probabilities: "probably it will be so," "most likely this," "expected that..." But a decision made from "probably" is not a decision from fact but a bet that may not pay off. Not because the method is bad, but because probability is a model, and truth is a fact.

**The second defect** consists in working inside uncertainty, not in exiting it. All methods try to manage uncertainty: reduce risk, calculate scenarios, estimate probabilities. But none offers to exit it. They act like an umbrella in the rain: they help not to get wet, but they do not cancel the rain. #WFS is not an umbrella but an exit from the rain, into a house where it is dry, not because the rain has ended, but because the person is not under it.

**The third defect** consists in ignoring the state of the decision-maker. All methods assume a rational subject who sees facts, weighs alternatives, and chooses the optimal. But the real person is not like that: they are fragmented, under archetypes, in paralysis. They do not see facts — they see interpretations; they do not weigh — they defend themselves; they do not choose — they fear. No method works with this and does not ask: "Who decides? In what state are they? What drives them?" Methods work with the task, not with the decision-maker, and therefore the distortion of the decision-maker is transmitted to the decision even when the method is ideal.

**2.5. The Trojan Horse of Reliability: The Substitution of DEV Machines in Translation**

**2.5.1. The Mechanism**

A probabilistic translator conveys content but shifts modality. A statement becomes an assumption, an order becomes advice, an obligation becomes an intention. Facts are preserved; the weight changes.

The reason is in the nature of the model: it is trained on plausibility, not on precision. A formulation with a hedge ("possibly," "probably," "should be considered") is statistically safer — it less often leads to conflict in the training data. The machine chooses the safe, and the imperative loses force.

For the recipient this means: before them is not a message but a version of the message. The author appears doubtful. Further actions are built on this impression. Below are model situations.

**2.5.2. Presidents and Diplomacy**

**Situation.** A head of state sends a note: "We demand the immediate withdrawal of forces from the territory. In case of refusal, retaliatory measures will follow." The translator conveys: "We would urge the withdrawal of forces. Should this not occur, retaliatory measures may be considered."

"We demand" became "urge." "Will follow" became "may be considered." The note reads as a request with a caveat. The opposing side draws a conclusion: there is no resolve, one can stall, one can bargain. Negotiations begin from a position of weakness that was not in the original. The price is concessions that could have been avoided, or escalation provoked by misreading.

**Second case.** A joint statement of allies is agreed upon verbatim. The translator softens one point: "We confirm commitment" becomes "We remain broadly committed." One ally reads this as a cooling and begins separate contacts with a third party. The alliance cracks; the cause is the adverb "broadly," which was not in the agreed text.

**2.5.3. Generals and Command of Troops**

**Situation.** A commander transmits to a multinational headquarters: "Hold the line until 06:00. Withdrawal is forbidden." The translator: "The line should be held until 06:00. Withdrawal is not recommended."

"Forbidden" became "not recommended." The order turned into a recommendation. The adjacent unit reads: holding is desirable, but otherwise is permissible. At 05:20 the line is abandoned. Forty minutes later the enemy passes through it. The cause is not cowardice and not an intelligence error — it is the modality of one verb.

**Second case.** Artillery adjustment: "Target confirmed. Fire for effect." The translator: "Target appears confirmed. Fire may be authorized." The crew waits for a clarification that will not come: communication is cut. The window closes, the target leaves and an hour later strikes. The losses are a consequence of the pause that arose from "may be."

**2.5.4. Doctors and Clinical Decisions**

**Situation.** The doctor on duty transmits to a colleague through a translator: "Urgent blood transfusion is indicated. The group is rare, the reserve is one unit." The translator: "Transfusion may be indicated. The blood type is rare; one unit is available."

"Is indicated" became "may be indicated." "The reserve is one" became "one unit is available" — a neutral description. The colleague reads: there is no urgency, there is a margin of time. The transfusion is postponed for forty minutes. During this time the condition deteriorates. The cause is not in the diagnosis and not in the drug, but in the softening of the indication.

**Second case.** The anesthesiologist warns: "The patient has an allergy to the drug. Do not administer." The translator: "The patient has a noted allergy. Alternative options should be considered." "Do not administer" became "should be considered" — a recommendation instead of a prohibition. In the absence of an alternative, the drug is administered. The reaction develops in the operating room. The cause is the word "noted," which gave the prohibition the character of a note.

**2.5.5. Judges and Court Proceedings**

**Situation.** A witness states through a translator: "I saw him raise his hand. I did not see the strike." The translator: "I believe I saw him raise his hand. I may not have seen the strike."

"Saw" became "I believe I saw." "Did not see" became "may not have seen." A testimony in which there was one firm fact and one firm absence turned into doubt about both. The judge reads: the witness is uncertain, therefore the testimony is unreliable. The prosecution is weakened, the case is reclassified. The cause is "believe" and "may," which were not in the witness's words.

**Second case.** The defendant states: "I was not in that area that evening." The translator: "I do not believe I was in that area that evening." "Was not" became "do not believe I was" — an admission of possibility. The prosecutor reads: the alibi is shaky. The court extends the measure of restraint for clarification. The person remains in custody because of the verb "believe" inserted by the translator.

**2.5.6. Rescuers and Emergency Services**

**Situation.** An international brigade works in an earthquake zone. The commander transmits: "Move away from the structure. A collapse is coming." The translator: "It may be advisable to move away. A collapse is possible."

"Move away" became "may be advisable." The adjacent link reads: a recommendation, not an order. Two people delay for thirty seconds to finish the extraction. The structure falls in twenty. The collapse was predicted, the order was given. Communication broke in the word "advisable."

**Second case.** The dispatcher transmits: "Evacuation. Immediately. Everyone — to the exit." The translator: "Evacuation is recommended. Everyone should proceed to the exit." Part of the staff decides to finish the shift — five minutes. In five minutes the smoke fills the corridor. The cause is not in the fire but in the replacement of "immediately" with "should."

**2.5.7. International Deal**

A buyer sends a message to a seller: "I will meet your cargo at the port. I will order and pay for the unloading equipment myself."

The DEV-AI translator renders: "I may be able to meet your cargo at the port. I will probably order and pay for the unloading equipment myself."

The facts have not changed. The modality has changed: a statement became an assumption. The seller reads not an obligation but an intention. Then follow the variants of what takes shape in their perception.

**Variant 1. Stop.** The buyer is not interested in the earliest shipment. Is in no hurry. The product can be queued for the next quarter.

**Variant 2. The buyer is unreliable.** Since they are not sure of their own actions, obligations may not be fulfilled on time. The seller begins to look for a backup buyer, disperses attention between two deals, loses focus. The result: either lost profit or conflict with both.

**Variant 3. The buyer is bargaining.** The softening is read as a signal to revise the terms. The seller prepares a discount that was not implied in the original message. The margin decreases without objective reasons.

**Variant 4. The buyer has no authority.** If they "may be able," then the decision is not in their hands. The seller demands access to another level, the deal is delayed in approvals, the window is lost.

**Variant 5. The buyer is bluffing.** Confidence is replaced by caution, which means there may be no readiness to pay behind the words. The seller demands prepayment or guarantees; the buyer perceives this as distrust. Relations deteriorate before work begins.

**Variant 6. The cargo is not needed.** Since the meeting is "possible" and the payment "probable," the interest is not confirmed. The seller sells the cargo to another. The buyer, whose interest was complete, is left without goods and without explanations.

In all variants the original message contained an obligation. Not one fact changed. Only the perception of the author changed: from a person who obligates, they turned into a person who assumes. The seller's further decisions are built on this transformation.

**2.5.8. General Mechanism and Price**

In all situations the fact is preserved. The modality is shifted: a statement became a probability, an order became a recommendation, a prohibition became a note, a testimony became doubt, an obligation became an intention.

The recipient judges not by content but by weight. The weight is substituted. Further, the decision is built on the substituted weight: the note is read as weakness, the order as advice, the prohibition as a caveat, the testimony as uncertainty, the obligation as planning.

The price differs by sphere: concessions in diplomacy, an abandoned line in the troops, a postponed transfusion in medicine, an extended arrest in court, the dead in a collapse, lost profit in business. The mechanism is one.

The substitution here is not malicious intent. The machine does not lie — it chooses a statistically safe form. The lie arises at the exit: the recipient sees the author not as they are, but as the DEV machine's formulation made them.

**2.5.9. Conclusion for the Reader**

Translation is not the transmission of words but the transmission of weight. If the weight is shifted, the message is distorted even when every word is translated correctly.

For the decision-maker this means: when working with a probabilistic translator, a critical message requires either mechanical translation without a model, or verification against the original, or confirmation from the author. Otherwise the decision is made by the version, not by the message.

**2.6. Critical "Surprises" of DEV-AI**

Artificial intelligence, which today is used as an assistant in decision-making, carries another special danger. It is not in the methods but in the tools, and it is lethal.

**Situation one. Court**

The system issues a report for a court. The document contains real data, requisites: court, address, phone, name of the judge, date. Everything is verifiable, everything matches reality. The person looks at the beginning — and trusts, because everything is real. In the middle of the document there is a reference to a decision that does not exist. The number is plausible, the wording is plausible, the signature is plausible.

The person checks the beginning — the requisites match. Trust is established. The middle cannot be checked: for this it is necessary to manually sort through thousands of decisions, which is physically impossible. The document is accepted as a basis, a lawsuit is filed.

The court checks — there is no decision. Consequence: the person who presented the document finds themselves in the position of one who misled the court. Responsibility falls on the person, not on the machine. The machine remains a statistical system.

The mechanism of the Trojan horse of reliability: a real facade, a fictional filling, the impossibility of verification. The price: reputation, legal costs, criminal risk. The damage in this episode exceeds possible intent.

The root of the above phenomena (2.5 and 2.6) is in the architecture of the machines that today make decisions. They are trained on probabilities, not on truth, and generate plausibility, not fact. OpenAI in the study "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (September 2025) admits: "Language models do not learn absolute truths, they learn probabilities." This is not a bug but an architecture: the machine has no internal criterion of truth and cannot say "this contradicts the facts." It says "this is statistically probable," and probability is not truth. A Stanford study (Nature Machine Intelligence, November 2025) on 24 LLMs and 13,000 questions confirms: GPT-4o falls from 98.2% to 64.4% when working with false beliefs, DeepSeek R1 — from >90% to 14.4%, and the average accuracy on false beliefs from the first person is 54.4%. The machine does not see the difference between "I know" and "I believe": for it this is the same pattern. And when a person trusts the machine, they trust probability, not truth.

**2.7. The Blind Spot of Preparation**

In institutes where administrators and civil servants are trained, they teach economics, law, management, methods of analysis and decision-making. The program is built on basic knowledge: how processes are structured, what criteria exist, how to calculate risks. This is a necessary minimum, and it is given. What is absent in this preparation is work with coherence. The state of the one who will make decisions is not part of the program — neither as a subject, nor as an exercise, nor as an admission criterion.

The reason is not in evil will and not in negligence. The teachers themselves are in the same state that they do not teach: their attention is fragmented, their coherence is low. A person cannot transmit what they do not have. They can transmit knowledge about coherence, but not coherence itself, because it is not transmitted by text but by state.

Hence the result. The graduate knows how to "correctly" make decisions and does not know how to be in the state in which a correct decision becomes possible. They leave with a set of methods and with the same fragmented attention with which they entered. And when they find themselves before real uncertainty, the methods do not work, because they are applied not by the ideal subject from the textbook but by the actual person — tired, frightened, hurried. This is the blind spot of preparation: it trains the instrument, but not the one who holds it.

**2.8. The Arsenal Is Not Bad, It Is Incomplete**

It is important to clarify: the arsenal is not useless. Classical criteria, probabilistic methods, and multi-criteria analysis work under certain conditions — when data are complete, probabilities are known, and the decision-maker is coherent. The problem is different: the arsenal does not cover those cases where there are no data, probabilities are unknown, and the decision-maker is fragmented. And it is precisely these cases that are most important, because the price of error in them is highest.

And here a void arises. The methods turn out to be inapplicable, and a decision is required immediately. It is precisely in this void that the next step begins: not "another method" in the same row, but a paradigm shift — work not with the process but with the state of the one who creates this process.
