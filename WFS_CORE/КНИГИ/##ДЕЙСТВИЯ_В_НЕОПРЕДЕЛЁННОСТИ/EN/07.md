**Part I. Statement**
**Chapter 1. Landscape**

**1.1. The Field in Which a Body of Practices Is Accumulated**

A field of knowledge has formed around the identification and elimination of losses in organizational processes. It contains a significant body of methodologies, metrics, and tools developed over decades. Participants in this field possess process maps, measurement systems, audit procedures, and trained specialists. Their activity targets the elimination of existing losses, not the increase of resources. The field has a documented history that includes both confirmed successes and recorded failures.

The purpose of this chapter is to show the systemic defect of the entire field by stating a precise diagnosis. The reader sees the root of the problem immediately, rather than reading the problem as an error of methods or executors. A literature review that merely lists schools does not state a diagnosis. Without a diagnosis, the reader becomes accustomed to the idea that these schools simply "underperformed," instead of seeing that they look in the wrong direction.

**1.2. Schools That Work with Losses**

Lean grew out of the Toyota Production System, formed by Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo in the 1950s–1970s. Its starting point is the elimination of non-productive costs (muda). Its toolkit includes kanban, just-in-time, 5S, value stream mapping, and kaizen. Its declared goal is the creation of value with lower resource expenditure.

Six Sigma offers a methodology for reducing variability and the number of defects through the DMAIC cycle (define, measure, analyze, improve, control). The methodology requires statistical analysis and institutionalized expertise at the black belt level.

Theory of Constraints (TOC), formulated by Eliyahu Goldratt, focuses on the constraint that determines the throughput of the entire system. The target of elimination is not the entire set of losses, but the single link that holds back the flow.

World Class Manufacturing (WCM) and Total Productive Maintenance (TPM) are complex systems for managing losses. The key metric is Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE), with a target level set at 85%.

Quality management systems, statistical process control, and organizational development approaches adjoin these schools. Each direction has its own toolkit and its own reporting system.

**1.3. Successes That Cannot Be Ignored**

It would be unfair to begin with criticism without acknowledging real results. Lean and WCM produced measurable improvements: in samples from dozens of production sites, OEE rose from 81.3% to 87.3% at specific facilities, and Zero-Based Budgeting cut overhead costs by up to 25%. In Russia, Rostec and KAMAZ achieved economic effects in the hundreds of millions of rubles at individual enterprises without large capital investments. Beyond the shop floor, in digital product creation flows, DevOps accelerated deployment by dozens of times and reduced failure rates. These figures are not opinion. They are fact.

The problem is not that the methods do not work. The problem is that they have a ceiling.

**1.4. The Systemic Limit**

The key indicator of the field is the share of failed initiatives. According to data cited by Lean and Six Sigma researchers, 60–70% of initiatives do not achieve their declared goals. Other estimates put the failure rate at 70–95% when the criterion is the absence of sustainable results. A separate indicator for continuous improvement programs: 96% of companies terminate such programs within 18 months.

The reasons cited by the researchers themselves reduce to several fundamental factors.

First — focus on tools, not on the state of people. Lean, Six Sigma, and TOC assume that people will apply the tool. But a person under the influence of archetypes, in a state of paralysis or chaotic activity, does not apply the tool. The tool is ineffective when the person applying it is in a state of fear.

Second — absence of work with internal uncertainty. These schools work with external losses: downtime, defects, inventory, cycle time. The root of losses is located not in the process, but in the decision-maker — in their attention, archetypes, and coherence. This root is not part of the analysis.

Third — instability of results. Even successful pilots lose stability after one to three years. The system changes the process but does not change the person. As soon as external pressure weakens, the person returns to previous patterns.

Fourth — training and certification are required. Lean, Six Sigma, and TOC represent investments in training, consultants, and the preparation of black-belt specialists. This is not a free tool.

Fifth — limited applicability in a turbulent environment. Six Sigma is rigidly tied to stable processes. In a rapidly changing environment, the method fails.

The combination of reasons points to one thing: the limit of the system is caused not by the quality of the tools, but by the fact that the state of the decision-maker is excluded from the analysis.

**1.5. The Root Defect: The Model of the Ideal Subject**

The root defect of all these schools is the model of the ideal rational subject who always applies the tool precisely and impartially. From this premise follows a second, less explicit one: the subject applying the method is treated as a functioning instrument. The subject is assumed to be capable of measuring without distortion, analyzing without bias, and applying the method without deviation. The subject's state is not included in the list of variables subject to measurement.

The methodologies are built on the premise of a rational subject acting in ideal conditions: possessing complete data, free from emotional distortions, capable of impartial calculation. This model is convenient for constructing formulas. It does not correspond to observed reality.

The actual person differs from this model. The person is subject to defensive distortions — not always and not necessarily maliciously, but prone to hiding errors and distorting reality because they are defending themselves. The person avoids risk — fears error, punishment, loss of face. The person acts not only from calculation — but from patterns, emotions, and experiences. And most importantly: their attention is not in the present. Attention is fragmented, the mind is occupied with past and future, and at the moment of decision the person is present only partially.

From this follows the consequence that statistics record. Initiatives fade over time because entropy absorbs them: one error triggers the next, rework accumulates, attention dissipates. The quality of decisions falls regardless of the quality of the method, because the decision-maker is fragmented.

The diagnosis of the field is: it models a subject that does not exist. Not because its authors are unreasonable or negligent. But because the model of "rational man" in these schools was never tested against "actual man."

**1.6. The Reason for the Invisibility of the Decision-Maker**

The invisibility of the decision-maker's state is caused by three factors: the model of the ideal subject, the localization of measurements only in external indicators, and the fragmented attention of the researchers themselves.

First basis — the model of the ideal subject. The methodologies are built on the premise of a rational subject acting in ideal conditions. This model is convenient for constructing formulas. It does not correspond to the state of phenomena.

Second basis — localization of measurement. Measurement is concentrated on what is subject to external fixation: operation time, number of defects, inventory volume, equipment effectiveness. The state of the decision-maker is not subject to external fixation in these systems because it has no metric. The absence of a metric is perceived as the absence of a phenomenon.

Third basis — the state of the researchers themselves. The researcher applying the method is not in the same state that they describe as the norm. The researcher's attention is fragmented, their coherence is low, the mind is occupied with the project, not with observation. In such a state, the blind spot is not detected because the detection instrument itself is subject to distortion.

The three bases combine into a stable picture: the defect exists but is not recorded; it is not recorded because there is no metric; there is no metric because the model of the subject excludes their state; the model excludes the state because the researcher does not observe their own.

**1.7. Consequence: The Divergence Between the Declared and the Actual**

From the root defect follows a divergence between the declared and actual effectiveness of the methods. In reporting, the methods demonstrate high indicators. In practical activity, a significant portion of initiatives does not achieve the declared results.

The divergence is explained by resistance of the environment, insufficient qualification of executors, or unfavorable circumstances. Such an explanation leaves the initial premise unchanged: the method is functional, the subject is functional, the environment is at fault.

When the decision-maker is included in the analysis, the explanation changes. The method can be functional, and the result still not achieved, because the application of the method occurs in a state where a significant part of attention is occupied by internal noise, not by the task. Losses in this case are localized not in the process, but in the decision-maker, and therefore are not visible to any reporting system.

**1.8. Four Levels of Uncertainty**

The four-level scale of uncertainty defines the boundaries of applicability of existing tools: determinism → risk → uncertainty → ignorance. At the last level, data are completely absent, which puts classical approaches out of operation.

Modern methods work at the first three levels. At the fourth they are powerless, because there are no data to rely on. And this level is the most important, because the price of error at it is highest: a president, a general, a leader, a doctor, a judge, and a rescuer cannot wait for complete data. They must decide now, with what they have, and with the state they are in.

**1.9. What Changes When the Decision-Maker Is Included in the Analysis**

Including the decision-maker in the analysis changes the formulation of the task. The question of which tool to apply is supplemented by the question of the state of the one applying it. The tool retains significance but ceases to be the only variable.

From this follows a redefinition of the subject of study. The subject becomes not only the process but also the state of the subject making the decision. Losses are considered in two localizations: in the process and in the decision-maker. Eliminating losses in the process without eliminating losses in the decision-maker produces a limited result, because the source of distortion remains.

This position does not cancel the listed schools. It indicates their place: tools retain force under the condition that the one applying them is in a state that permits applying them accordingly.

If this diagnosis is not stated immediately, the entire book reads as a dispute about methods. If it is stated, the book is perceived as pointing to the blind spot of an entire field. This is why the next step is not "another method" in the same row, but a change of level: work not with the process, but with the state of the one who creates this process.
