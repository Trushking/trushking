**Chapter 17. Battlefield**

**17.1. Map and Trenches**

An image: a field. Not a map on a table. A field with mud, trenches, smoke, and people. The decision is made not in an office. But here, under fire, in uncertainty. Where an error means not a line in a report but lives.

**17.2. Auftragstaktik — Mission-Oriented Command**

This is a concept of Prussian-German origin: Auftragstaktik. It is defined as trust, not as micromanagement. The commander sets the goal: what to do, not how. The subordinate decides how, since they are on the spot and see reality. The commander is far away and does not know the details.

There are three principles.

**Responsibility.** Each commander bears full responsibility for their zone.

**Trust.** The commander trusts the subordinate; the subordinate acts on initiative.

**Unified intent.** All understand the common goal, each solves their own task.

Auftragstaktik manifests as #ZR in military affairs. Clear zone. Clear goal. Freedom in methods. An order defining the goal. How — is decided by the one on the spot, in reality, not in headquarters.

**17.3. Success: Clarity of #ZR**

**Hamel, 1918.** World War I. Australian General John Monash. Attack with tanks, infantry, artillery, aviation. Each commander knew their zone and the common goal. Did not wait for orders. Acted. Result: German positions taken in 93 minutes. Losses — moderate for that war. Cause — the work of #ZR.

**Operation Phantom Fury, 2004.** Iraq. General Natonski. Mission command. Commanders on the ground had freedom within the unified intent. Did not wait for instructions. Decided. Result: one of the most decisive victories of the Iraq War. Basis — clarity.

**Chipyong-ni, 1951.** Korea. General Ridgway. Shared understanding and mutual trust. Forces repelled the Chinese attack because each commander knew their task and acted without orders. Cause — #ZR.

**USS San Francisco, 2005.** Submarine. Accident. The commander and crew acted according to protocols without panic. Each knew their zone. The boat survived despite heavy damage. Root — clarity.

Common: clarity of #ZR. Each knew: what to do, where, when. And did it. In their zone. With the common goal.

**17.4. Failure: Absence of Clarity**

**River Raisin, 1813.** Failure of mission command. Absence of a clear intent, ineffective communication, absence of mutual trust. #ZR — chaos. Defeat.

**Gettysburg, 1863.** US Civil War. General Meade did not pursue Lee after victory because he had no clarity in orders and intelligence. The Confederate Army survived and fought for two more years. Cause — chaos; #ZR was not clear.

**Dieppe, 1942.** Raid. Absence of a single commander. Naval, air, and ground components were not coordinated. Losses — over 60% of the landing force. Basis — absence of #ZR. Each in their own zone, no common picture.

**Mogadishu, 1993.** Somalia. Absence of command control and limited resources. Each acted in their own zone, did not understand the common picture. #ZR not defined. Losses.

**Wanat, 2008.** Afghanistan. Absence of mutual trust and disciplined initiative. Commanders did not trust each other and did not take responsibility. #ZR blocked.

Common: absence of clarity of #ZR. Chaos. Each in their own zone, no common picture. Catastrophe.
